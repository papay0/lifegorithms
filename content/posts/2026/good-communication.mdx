## Listening is just as important as speaking

Good communication is not only about expressing yourself clearly. Person A to Person B. To enable the other person to communicate well, I need to be a good listener. It sounds obvious when you say it out loud, but it's worth being intentional about.

Have you ever felt truly heard? Someone looking you in the eye, nodding, fully present with you?

Now think of the opposite. Someone physically there but clearly not listening. Scrolling their phone. Waiting for their turn to speak. You can feel the difference immediately.

During the workshop, we practiced both. The contrast was striking. When someone truly listens, I feel cared for. That's not a small thing.

Being a good listener isn't passive. It's active. It's looking at the person. It's nodding. It's small verbal cues like "mmm" or "I see." It's resisting the urge to jump in with your own story or solution. It's creating space for the other person to feel heard.

## The Imago Dialogue

This one blew my mind. The Imago Dialogue is a structured way of having a conversation that completely changes how you listen and respond. It comes from couples therapy but works in any relationship.

Let's say Alice and Bob are in a relationship. They have a rule: clean the dishes within two hours after cooking. But Bob sometimes doesn't, and it bothers Alice. She decides to bring it up.

Instead of Bob jumping in with his opinion or waiting for his turn to talk, he goes through four steps:

**1. Mirror**

Alice shares how she feels. Bob repeats back exactly what he heard, without adding or interpreting: "What I'm hearing is that..."

Then he asks: "Is that right?"

If Alice says yes, Bob follows up with: "Is there more?"

He keeps doing this until Alice says no, there's nothing more.

**2. Summarize**

Once Alice is done, Bob summarizes everything she said. "Let me see if I got it all. When I leave the dishes overnight, you feel disrespected, and you feel like you're the only one keeping the house in order. Is that right?"

This ensures he captured the full picture before moving on.

**3. Validate**

Now Bob validates. This means acknowledging that Alice's perspective makes sense, even if he doesn't fully agree.

He might say: "That makes sense. I can see how me not following through on something we agreed on would make you feel that way."

This is not about agreeing. It's about recognizing that her experience is real and valid.

**4. Empathize**

Finally, Bob tries to step into Alice's emotional experience. He imagines how she might be feeling and reflects that back.

Something like: "I imagine that feels frustrating. Maybe even lonely."

This is where connection happens. Not in fixing, not in advising, but in truly seeing the other person.

**The full dialogue**

> **Alice:** "When you leave the dishes in the sink overnight, I feel disrespected. Like the rule we agreed on doesn't matter to you."
>
> **Bob (mirroring):** "What I'm hearing is that when I don't do the dishes, you feel like I don't care about our agreement. Is that right?"
>
> **Alice:** "Not exactly. It's not that you don't care. It's that I feel disrespected."
>
> **Bob (mirroring again):** "What I'm hearing is that when I leave the dishes, you feel disrespected. Is that right?"
>
> **Alice:** "Yes."
>
> **Bob:** "Is there more?"
>
> **Alice:** "Yes. It also makes me feel like I'm the only one maintaining the house."
>
> **Bob (mirroring):** "What I'm hearing is that it makes you feel like you're the only one maintaining the house. Is that right?"
>
> **Alice:** "Yes."
>
> **Bob:** "Is there more?"
>
> **Alice:** "No, that's it."
>
> **Bob (summarizing):** "Let me see if I got it all. When I leave the dishes overnight, you feel disrespected, and you also feel like you're the only one keeping the house in order. Is that right?"
>
> **Alice:** "Yes."
>
> **Bob (validating):** "That makes sense. I can see how me not following through on something we agreed on would make you feel that way."
>
> **Bob (empathizing):** "I imagine that feels frustrating. Maybe even lonely."

Notice how Bob got it wrong the first time, and Alice corrected him. That's the point.

> The process catches misunderstandings before they snowball.

## Hug, Help, or Heard

When someone comes to you with a problem, what's your first instinct? For most of us, it's to fix it. We jump into solution mode. We give advice. We try to help.

But sometimes that's not what the person needs at all.

The idea is simple: before responding, ask them what they need. Do they need a hug? Do they need help? Or do they just need to be heard?

These are three very different things:

- **Hug**: They need comfort, warmth, reassurance. Not words, just presence.
- **Help**: They actually want you to help solve the problem. Advice is welcome.
- **Heard**: They just need to vent. They don't want solutions. They want you to listen.

Most frustration in conversations comes from a mismatch. Someone wants to be heard, but you're offering help. Someone needs a hug, but you're asking questions. By simply asking what they need, you skip the guessing and meet them where they are.

**Back to Alice and Bob**

Alice comes home frustrated after a hard day at work. She starts telling Bob about a conflict with her manager.

Bob's instinct is to help. "Have you tried talking to HR?" he says. "Maybe you should document everything."

Alice gets more frustrated. She didn't want a solution. She just wanted to vent.

Now imagine Bob asks first: "That sounds rough. Do you want me to just listen, or do you want help thinking through it?"

Alice says: "I just need to vent."

Bob nods. He listens. He doesn't fix. And Alice feels better.

> Sometimes the most helpful thing you can do is not help at all.



## "The story I'm making up"

This one comes from Brené Brown's book *Rising Strong*, and it's an extra step on top of Nonviolent Communication (NVC).

In NVC, when you want to express something, you typically share: the facts, your feelings, and your needs. But Brené adds something powerful: the story.

The story is the narrative your brain creates to fill in the gaps. It's often wrong. But we treat it like truth and react to it.

The format looks like this:

1. **Facts**: What actually happened
2. **The story I'm making up**: The narrative in your head (might not be true)
3. **Feelings**: What emotions come up
4. **Asks**: What you need

By saying "the story I'm making up," you're signaling two things: first, that you're aware it might not be true. Second, you're giving the other person a window into your head so they can correct it if needed.

**Back to Alice and Bob**

Let's go back to the dishes. Alice could say:

> "When you leave the dishes in the sink overnight, the story I'm making up is that you don't love me. I feel disrespected and unimportant. What I need is for us to follow the rule we agreed on."

Now, "Bob doesn't love me" is clearly a made-up story. Alice probably knows that. But by saying it out loud, two things happen:

1. **Alice becomes aware** that it's just a story. It's not a fact. It might not be true at all.
2. **Bob now understands** how Alice's mind works. He sees the gap between what happened and what she felt. He can reassure her: "I do love you. I was just tired and forgot. It has nothing to do with how I feel about you."

Without that step, Alice might have just said "I feel disrespected" and Bob would have no idea that her brain jumped to "he doesn't love me." The made-up story stays hidden, and the real fear never gets addressed.


## "I feel" instead of "It makes me feel"

This one is subtle but important.

There's a difference between:

- "It makes me feel disrespected"
- "I feel disrespected"

The first one says: something is doing this to me. It's subtle blame.

The second one just states the emotion. No blame. Just: this is how I feel.

**Back to Alice and Bob**

Instead of: "It makes me feel like I don't matter when the dishes are left overnight."

Alice says: "I feel like I don't matter when the dishes are left overnight."

Same feeling. But there's no blame attached. Alice is simply describing her internal experience. Bob can hear it without feeling like he's being accused of something.

It's a small shift in language. But it changes everything about how the conversation goes.

> Own your feelings. Don't assign them.

## Conclusion

None of this is complicated. Listen fully. Mirror before responding. Ask what they need. Name your stories. Own your feelings.

But simple doesn't mean easy. It takes practice. It takes slowing down when your instincts want to speed up.

The payoff is worth it. Better conversations. Fewer misunderstandings. Deeper connection. And the rare feeling of being truly understood.
